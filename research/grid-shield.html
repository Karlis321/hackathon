Grid Shield: drone screens for small critical infrastructure, built from dismantled grid material

A business that turns Sadales tīkls' dismantled poles and cable offcuts into protection kits for small, critical objects. This page covers which objects to target, who buys and why, and how the idea scores against the jury's criteria.

The business in one picture

We take material the grid company has to get rid of and sell it back as a finished protection kit, installed and maintained, for objects small enough that a screen around them is cheap and quick to build.

The process: six simple steps

All we need from a cable is the wire inside. So every piece follows the same path, whatever its type, size, length or condition.

  1. 1Collect

    Crews throw any cable offcut into one Grid Shield bin at the depot. We swap full bins for empty ones.

    Sadales tīkls · no new work
  2. 2Strip

    Take the insulation off. Burnt or damaged cable is fine.

    Stripping knife · insulation → recycler
  3. 3Separate

    Scratch the metal. Orange means copper, which goes to ties. Silver means aluminium, which goes to the net. A thick solid core becomes slat bars.

    Knife scratch · 3 bins
  4. 4Cut

    Cut the wires on a bench with stops at 2 m and 1 m. Short leftovers become rings or ties.

    Cutting bench
  5. 5Weave

    Weave the sticks over and under on a peg board into 1 × 1 m panels, with no joints.

    Peg board · 1–2 people
  6. 6Install

    Clip the panels onto a rope frame on the reused wooden posts around the transformer. Earth the mesh. Inspect it yearly.

    3-person crew · ~4 days

Only three outcomes from step 3

Aluminium wire→ the net (steps 4–5)
Thick solid core→ slat bars in front of the radiators
Copper wire→ ties and lashing

Copper and aluminium are kept apart because they corrode where they touch. Only metal that has melted or crumbles goes to the recycler.

Poles alongside

  • Wooden poles: cut each one in two to make the posts.
  • Concrete poles: crush them into stone to fill a low wall at the base. The steel inside goes to scrap.

Crews hand these over as they do today.

Which small objects to protect

These are critical objects that fit inside a screen of tens of metres. Large sites (power plants, ports, the whole gas storage site) are left out: they need military-grade protection, not a kit. Each object is scored from 0 to 10 on six criteria. Military sites are left out on purpose.

The six criteria: Critical, how bad losing it is. Fit, how well a small screen kit fits its size and shape. Exposure, how remote, unguarded or close to the border it is. Volume, how many there are, which is the market size. Safety, how easy it is to work around (10 means no high voltage nearby). Payer, how clear and able to pay the owner is.

Recommendation

  • Lead product: 110/20 kV substation transformers. Critical, a clear payer (the challenge owner itself), and enough of them to be a market.
  • Showcase pilot: the AST battery storage in Rēzekne (60 MW). It's a new, €77M asset about 40 km from the Russian border, made of low containers that are easy to screen.
  • Easiest second market: gas regulating stations and the 20/0.4 kV kiosks that feed hospitals and water plants. There's no 110 kV clearance problem and there are many of them.

    Reference object: one 110/20 kV transformer

    A single transformer at a small substation in open country. It's about 7 × 5 m on a concrete foundation with an oil pit and lattice masts. Most substations have two. The weak points are the radiators and tank walls, where a puncture leaks oil and takes the unit out. The bushings on top sit inside the 110 kV clearance zone, so nothing can be fixed near them.

    What it stops, and what it doesn't

    • Stops: FPV and small commercial drones with a small charge, and flying debris.
    • Does not stop: Shahed-class drones (about 50 kg warhead) or missiles.
    • Version 1 covers the sides only. The conductors come down into the bushings from above, so a roof needs openings.

    Safety rules built into the design

    • Earthing: the metal mesh is earthed and kept outside the electrical clearance zone.
    • No Al–Cu contact: spacers keep the two metals apart so they don't corrode.
    • Screens only: soft aluminium is never used in load-bearing parts. The gabion baskets are steel.
    • Theft: the outdoor mesh is aluminium, which is worth little as scrap.

    The net: stripped metal for the mesh, whole cable for the ropes

    Anti-FPV nets use 20–45 mm mesh. Commercial drone net is 2–3 mm polypropylene twine rated at 70–130 kg. Whole MV and overhead cables are 25–45 mm thick and bend no tighter than about 0.5 m, so a net made from them has 30–50 cm holes that a drone flies through. The mesh therefore has to be woven from stripped wire. Whole cable is ideal for the frame ropes.

    Six ways to build one net from mixed cable sizes

    The trick is to give every size a job at the scale it suits, instead of forcing all cables into the same weave.

    1. Three-tier net (recommended)

    The same layout as a fishing net or a spider web:

    • Tier 1, thick: whole AMKA and XL cable as the main ropes, in about 1 m cells. They carry all the load and tie into the posts.
    • Tier 2, medium: M-class strands, or whole thin cables, as a sub-grid of about 20 cm. This already stops most FPV drones on its own.
    • Tier 3, fine: 40–50 mm mesh woven from L-class strands fills each cell. It only has to span 20 cm, so short strands are enough.

    If a fine cell tears, the sub-grid still holds, and the repair is one cell, not a whole panel.

    2. Standard modular panels

    Every panel is the same size, for example 1 × 2 m, with a frame of thick conductor bars (L/XL). Inside, the infill uses whatever strands are in stock that week. The outside is standard and the inside is flexible, so size variation never reaches the installer. Panels clip onto the rope grid, and a damaged one is swapped in minutes, which makes the repair contract cheap to run.

    3. Twist thin wires into one standard twine

    Twist several thin wires into one strand of the target strength. That's how the cable was made in the first place. The target is about 6–7 mm² of metal, equal to one 2.9 mm Al strand. For example: 4 × 1.5 mm² copper cores, or 3 × 2.5 mm² Al strands from AMKA 3×16. A drill-driven wire twister does it on site. Any mix of sizes becomes one uniform twine, so the weaving machine never sees the difference.

    4. Whole thin cable as a 10 cm grid

    Thin LV cables (NYY-J, (N)YM, AXMK 4×16) are 8–15 mm thick and bend down to a 5–9 cm radius. Woven over-under like a basket, they make a ~10 cm grid without stripping anything. A typical 5-inch FPV drone is about 20 cm across, so this grid stops it. It suits the middle tier or a standalone screen on small kiosks. The insulation also keeps the copper out of sight of thieves.

    5. Reuse the line hardware too

    Dismantled AMKA lines come with their own suspension clamps, anchor clamps and insulation-piercing connectors. Hang the AMKA rope on the wooden posts with the same clamps it used on the line. The net frame is then literally an overhead line rebuilt with a net hanging from it, which grid crews already know how to build. Where aluminium meets copper, the grid's own Al–Cu bimetal connectors solve the corrosion problem.

    6. Layer by size: slats outside, mesh inside

    Two screens about 0.5 m apart. The outer layer is slat bars from the shortest thick pieces, spaced about 8–10 cm, to stop or set off the drone. The inner layer is fine mesh to catch fragments. Heavy pieces go low, where impact and debris are worst. Light mesh goes high, to keep the load on the posts down.

    Combined, these give one design rule: thick cable carries the load, medium cable holds the shape, thin wire fills the gaps. Whatever mix comes in from Sadales tīkls, there is always a place for it, and the finished net looks the same every time.

    Mixed lengths: cut to standard sticks, never join

    Joining hundreds of short pieces is slow, and every joint is a weak point. The easier route is to avoid joints altogether. Cut every strand into a few standard lengths, and make each panel exactly as wide as one stick. Leftovers drop down to the next smaller size, so nothing is wasted.

    How a panel is made: a peg board, no machine

    • A 1 × 2 m board with pegs every 5 cm round the edge.
    • Lay the sticks one way, then weave the cross sticks over and under. A 1 × 1 m panel at 5 cm takes 20 + 20 one-metre sticks, about 40 m of wire.
    • Bend each stick end round the panel frame (a thick conductor bar), and twist a short wire from bin D at the crossings near the edge.
    • Panels clip onto the rope grid of the three-tier net.

    One or two people with pliers, no chain-link machine. It can start at the hackathon with a sample panel.

    When volume grows: cold welding

    For machine weaving, short strands can be joined end to end into one long wire with a cold pressure welder. This is a hand tool from the wire-drawing industry that squeezes two wire ends together, with no heat, flux or electricity. The joint is about as strong as the wire, and it also works for aluminium to aluminium and copper to copper. The joined coil then goes into a standard chain-link machine.

    Start with peg-board panels, and add cold welding and a machine once orders justify it. Check the welder's diameter range against the 2.5–3.6 mm Al strands.

    Thick ropes: use the grid's own joints

    When an AMKA rope is shorter than a span, join it with the full-tension splice sleeves grid crews already use on overhead lines. Same tool, same crew skill. A rope only needs a joint every few spans, so this costs little.

    What this means for the business: the mesh volume depends on how many metres of L-class aluminium (120–240 mm²) offcuts come in each year. The stock list only gives type names, so the first data request to Sadales tīkls is offcut length and metres per year for each type. Sorting by size at intake (XS–S, M–L, XL) sends each piece straight to the right line.

    How the business makes money

    First customer

    AS "Sadales tīkls"

    Supplier and customer at once. Today it pays to dispose of poles and broken concrete, and it would pay again for any new protection. We give it a single contract covering both.

    • Lower disposal bill: we take the material at a lower fee than disposal costs, or for free.
    • Protection for less than concrete: a screen costs a fraction of a concrete shelter or a hardened substation. Lithuania's Gižai substation costs €48M+ extra.
    • Legal duty: the EU CER Directive requires designated critical entities to take resilience measures. Our kit is a ready, documented measure.
    • Downtime avoided: a large transformer takes years to replace.
    Next customers

    AST · Conexus / Gaso · LVRTC · Tet · LMT · water utilities

    The owners of the other objects in the scoring table. Later come the other Baltic grid operators: Litgrid and ESO in Lithuania, Elering in Estonia. They face the same threat with the same equipment.

    How customers fund their own security spending is their business. Some use their own capex, some get EU co-funding. We sell to them and don't apply for grants ourselves.

    Market size (hypothesis)

    • Latvia, transformers: ~250–300 × €20k ≈ €5–6M one-off, plus ~€0.5M a year in service.
    • Latvia, other small objects (gas stations, kiosks at critical consumers, telecom, water): several hundred more units at lower prices.
    • Baltics: about 3× the Latvian transformer market if the grids are similar in size. That's an assumption to check.
    • Material supply caps growth: full Latvian coverage needs about 2,000 wooden poles and 20 km of offcuts. 444 poles were reused in the grid in 2026 to date. Plan for a phased rollout.

    Financing the company

    • A paid pilot with Sadales tīkls: 2–5 transformers, ideally with an upfront payment to cover the first stripping and weaving equipment.
    • Revenue-backed equipment financing, bank loan or leasing, for the stripping line and the chain-link machine.
    • Equity, once there are paying customers: defence and dual-use investors are actively looking. The NATO Innovation Fund (€1bn) names counter-drone as an urgent gap, and UniLab, which co-organises this Climathon, runs Latvia's NATO DIANA accelerator site.

    Evidence from Ukraine

    • Substations: Ukrenergo uses three protection levels. First gabions and sandbags (90 facilities), then concrete shelters around transformers (22 substations), then anti-missile protection. The EIB lent it €86M for this.
    • Nets: 822 km of roads were netted in 2025, with 4,000 km targeted for 2026. Nets were also put up around substations. Much of the mesh is donated fishing net.
    • Limits: repeated hits tear the mesh, and drones find the gaps. This is why we sell an inspection and repair contract.
    • Baltic precedent: Lithuania's Litgrid is hardening the Gižai substation against drones for €48M+ extra.
